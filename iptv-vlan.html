IPTV Single Cable Multiplexing

Posted on 2026-07-23 Edited on 2026-07-28 In Free Internet Word count in article: 1.9k Reading time ≈ 7 mins.
My router used to sit next to the weak current box, which is pretty much the worst possible location. The Wi-Fi signal would randomly drop whenever I was lying in bed using my phone and happened to turn around. Eventually, I had enough and decided to move the router. The living room TV cabinet would be the perfect spot, but there was only one Ethernet cable running from the weak current box to the living room, and it was already occupied by IPTV. Moving the router meant making that single cable carry both Internet and IPTV traffic at the same time — a setup commonly known as single cable multiplexing.

Preparation

This setup requires:

  • 1 managed switch. I used a TP-Link SG-2005 in this article, which costs around 80 CNY
  • 1 router with IPTV configuration support. I used an ASUS AC66U-B1
  • 2-3 new Ethernet cables for connecting the switch to the optical modem and the router to the IPTV set-top box

Before getting into the configuration, here is some background information. If you are only interested in the actual setup, feel free to skip directly to the switch configuration section.

In most cases, ISP-provided optical modems handle broadband connections in router mode, while IPTV runs in bridge mode. One possible solution is to ask your ISP to switch broadband to bridge mode, then configure IPTV single cable multiplexing so both broadband and IPTV use the same LAN port on the optical modem. This removes the need for an additional switch.

The problem is that some ISPs simply refuse to enable bridge mode. I did not want to spend hours arguing with customer service, so I decided to add a managed switch and use VLANs to multiplex broadband and IPTV over a single Ethernet cable.

If your router supports IPTV VLAN configuration, you only need one managed switch in the weak current box. Otherwise, you will need another managed switch in the living room. In practice, an IPTV-capable router is basically taking over the job of that second managed switch.

PVID and VID

PVID and VID

PVID applies to incoming traffic, while VID applies to outgoing traffic. This can be confusing at first, so let’s walk through how traffic actually moves through the switch.

  • Incoming broadband traffic, for example, data returned from a website when your phone opens a page:
    1. The optical modem receives Internet traffic from the ISP. The internal processing inside the optical modem is not relevant here.
    2. Untagged traffic leaves the optical modem through LAN1.
    3. The untagged traffic reaches switch port 2.
    4. Since the traffic does not have a VLAN tag, the switch assigns it to VLAN2 according to the port’s PVID setting (PVID=2).
    5. Port 1 is configured as Tagged for VLAN2, so the switch adds a VLAN tag (VID=2) before sending the traffic out.
    6. Tagged traffic (VID=2) leaves the switch through port 1.
    7. The tagged traffic reaches the WAN port of the router.
    8. The router recognizes VLAN ID 2, processes the traffic as Internet traffic through routing/NAT, then removes the tag and sends it internally as untagged traffic.
    9. The untagged traffic is finally delivered to the phone through Wi-Fi.
  • Traffic sent from the phone back to the Internet:
    1. The phone sends untagged traffic to the router.
    2. The router identifies it as Internet traffic and adds the configured VLAN tag (VID=2) before sending it through the WAN port.
    3. Tagged traffic (VID=2) leaves the router WAN port.
    4. The tagged traffic reaches switch port 1.
    5. Since the switch receives already-tagged traffic, it does not use the port PVID and directly assigns the traffic to VLAN2.
    6. VLAN2 port 2 is configured as Untagged, so the switch removes the VLAN tag before forwarding the traffic.
    7. Untagged traffic leaves the switch through port 2.
    8. The traffic reaches LAN1 of the optical modem.
    9. The optical modem forwards the data back to the ISP network, eventually reaching the website.

The confusing part here is the relationship between VLAN tags and the traditional concepts of Trunk and Access ports. Their relationship is:

  • Tagged is equivalent to Trunk
  • Untagged is equivalent to Access
  • An Access-style port can only belong to one VLAN, and traffic leaving that port does not contain VLAN tags
  • A Trunk-style port can carry multiple VLANs, and traffic from that port is tagged
  • A Trunk port only allows VLAN traffic with matching tags to pass, which means multiple tagged VLANs can share the same port
  • An Access VLAN port only allows one Access VLAN to pass through

How to Check Wi-Fi Signal Strength on iPhone

How to Check Wi-Fi Signal Strength on iPhone

The Wi-Fi icon in the iPhone status bar is intentionally conservative. Even at -75 dBm, it can still show two bars, while the actual transmission rate may already be significantly reduced. The icon alone is not a reliable way to judge Wi-Fi quality.

Fortunately, you do not need any third-party apps. Apple provides a configuration profile that allows you to view detailed Wi-Fi diagnostics.

  • Register as an Apple Developer with your Apple ID through Apple Developer Registration, then open Profiles and Logs - Feedback Assistant - Apple Developer
  • Scroll to the bottom and download the Wi-Fi for iOS/iPadOS profile
  • Install and enable the profile on your iPhone
  • Go to Settings → Wi-Fi and tap the ℹ️ icon next to your connected network
  • Tap Diagnostics at the top to view detailed Wi-Fi information

Switch Configuration

The first step is accessing the switch’s management interface.

The traditional way is to connect a computer to any port on the switch with an Ethernet cable, manually configure the computer’s IP address and subnet mask, and then open the switch management page.

Another option is to connect the optical modem’s broadband port to switch port 1 first, then use TP-Link’s cloud service to configure the VLAN settings. The following steps use the traditional web interface as an example. The cloud configuration process is almost identical.

Computer Network Configuration

This example uses Windows 10. First, change the IP address and subnet mask of the wired network adapter:

  1. Open Control Panel → Network and Internet → Network and Sharing Center → Change adapter settings
  2. Right-click the Ethernet connection and select Properties
  3. Open Internet Protocol Version 4 (TCP/IPv4)
  4. Manually configure the IP address and subnet mask, for example:
    • IP: 10.18.18.18
    • Subnet mask: 255.255.255.0
  5. Open the switch management page at 10.18.18.251 and set a password. The default username is admin

Enable 802.1Q VLAN

  1. Open VLAN → 802.1Q VLAN
  2. Enable 802.1Q VLAN
  3. Click Apply
  4. Confirm the popup dialog

Creating VLANs

VLAN1 exists by default, so we can leave it untouched for now. We only need to create three additional VLANs. Enter the VLAN ID and description, configure the port membership, and click Add.

One small thing worth mentioning: after changing the VLAN settings, make sure to click the Add/Edit button at the bottom instead of the Apply button at the top. I made this mistake the first time and spent quite a while wondering why nothing changed, thinking I had found a bug.

  • Broadband
    • VLAN ID: 2
    • Set port 1 as Tagged and port 2 as Untagged. Leave all other ports as Non-member.
  • IPTV
    • VLAN ID: 3
    • Set port 1 as Tagged and port 3 as Untagged. Leave all other ports as Non-member.
  • Other LAN (optional)
    • VLAN ID: 4
    • Set port 1 as Tagged and ports 4 and 5 as Untagged. Leave all other ports as Non-member.

Configure PVID

Set each port’s PVID to match the corresponding VLAN ID.

Change Management VLAN

By default, the management VLAN is VLAN1, while broadband traffic uses VLAN2. This creates a problem: after the configuration is complete, you will no longer be able to access the switch management interface through TP-Link’s cloud service. You would have to connect a computer directly to the switch with an Ethernet cable.

To fix this:

  • Go to System Management → IP Management → IP Setting
  • Change the Management VLAN to 2
  • Enable DHCP Settings, and click Apply.

After that, you can access the switch management interface through TP-Link’s cloud service.

Manage VLAN1 Ports

Remove all ports except port 1 from VLAN1 by setting them as Non-member.

This prevents unexpected problems, such as wired mesh network loops or IPTV connection issues.

Loop Prevention

Go to Monitoring → Loop Prevention, enable Loop Prevention and apply the settings.

QoS

Go to Quality of Service → QoS Configuration, set the QoS mode to Based on 802.1P.

Router Configuration

  • Open the router management page at 192.168.50.1
  • Go to LAN → IPTV, then configure the Internet and IPTV VLAN IDs. They must match the VLAN IDs configured on the switch.
  • PRIO represents traffic priority. I recommend setting IPTV priority to 5 and leaving Internet at the default value of 0.
  • The DHCP route option needs to use RFC3442, otherwise IPTV may fail to establish a connection. It is recommended to select RFC3442 & Microsoft directly.
  • If you only use the ISP-provided set-top box for IPTV, there is no need to worry about multicast routing and other options. I left those settings unchanged in my screenshot because they are not required in this setup. Enabling them does not affect normal usage, but changing these options requires a router reboot.

For more details about these settings:
ASUS Official Support - How to Set Up IPTV on Wireless Routers

Wiring

The final step is connecting everything together.

  • Weak current box
    • Connect optical modem LAN1 (the broadband port) to switch port 2
    • Connect optical modem LAN2 (the IPTV port) to switch port 3
    • Connect the Ethernet cable going from the weak current box to the living room wall outlet to switch port 1
  • Living room
    • Connect the wall outlet to the router’s WAN port
    • Connect router LAN3 or LAN4 (depending on your router configuration) to the IPTV set-top box

A Few Small Things I Had to Fix

I originally bought the switch and new Ethernet cables, ready for a proper network upgrade. However, when I opened the weak current box, I discovered that the construction team had split the cable going to the living room wall outlet into two separate ends during renovation.

They probably meant well, but the result was that the Ethernet link was only capable of 100 Mbps. So I had to re-terminate the cable. Not exactly the surprise I was hoping for.

The cable left inside the weak current box was also extremely short, making it difficult to work with. In the end, I gave up on using a crimping tool and installed a tool-free RJ45 module instead, then connected another short Ethernet cable from the module to the switch.

This makes future maintenance much easier. Otherwise, every time I needed to work on the connection, I would have to stick my hands deep into the box like a miner digging through a tunnel.
